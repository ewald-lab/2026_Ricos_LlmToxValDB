Abstract
Preclinical toxicology study reports contain the expert interpretations required to distinguish test article-related effects
from incidental findings, yet these conclusions often remain embedded in unstructured text form that limit systematic
reuse and integration with computational safety approaches. To address this gap, we developed a Large Language Model
(LLM) - supported pipeline that converts toxicology reports into structured, machine-readable datasets harmonized with
SEND terminology. The pipeline combines automated document preprocessing, section identification, schema-constrained
information extraction, and semantic harmonization, complemented by targeted human curation. We evaluated the system
performance using 200 Roche toxicology study reports, encompassing clinical pathology, histopathology, organ weights,
exposure data, and study-level conclusions. Across domains, extraction performance was strong, characterized by consistently high sensitivity and precision for most parameters. Histopathology, organ weight, and NOAEL-related endpoints
demonstrated the greatest robustness, with sensitivity typically above 95% and precision frequently exceeding 97%. Lower
performance for parameters such as route of administration and substance identifiers reflected heterogeneous reporting
practices rather than LLM-based method limitations. The structured datasets generated by this pipeline enable crossstudy querying, identification of compounds with defined toxicological liabilities, integration with raw SEND data, and
development of high-quality labels for predictive toxicology models. Practical utility has been demonstrated through
representative use cases. These results demonstrate that LLM-assisted extraction can reliably capture expert toxicological interpretations at scale and provide a foundation for data-centric safety assessment, strategic decisions, reverse and
forward translational toxicology research.

Introduction
Preclinical animal toxicology studies generate extensive
data crucial for the safety assessment of drugs and drug
candidates. While the Standard for Exchange of Nonclinical Data (SEND) provides structured formats for recording subject-level data, it is insufficient for robust predictive
modeling and comprehensive database searching: SEND
data primarily captures individual animal measurements,
lacking the crucial context of overall study interpretation. In
contrast, study reports contain vital information regarding
test article-related effects, distinguishing true toxicological
findings from incidental or spontaneous observations like
background findings. This valuable information, however, 
1 3
Archives of Toxicology
is locked within these unstructured free-text toxicology
reports, often available only as PDF documents. Without
access to these interpreted conclusions, building accurate
and predictive machine learning models and performing
meaningful database searches for structure-activity relationship analyses in computational toxicology become significantly hampered.
Major collaborative initiatives sought to address these
challenges. The eTOX consortium pioneered the capturing
of test article-related findings from in vivo toxicity study
reports in a machine-readable format through manual curation and ontology mapping of historical study data (Briggs
et al. 2012). The eTOX consortium was a public-private
partnership created to share preclinical data for the development of predictive in silico models. Pharmaceutical member
companies of the consortium captured findings by manually curating decades of legacy repeat-dose toxicity study
reports. This previously unstructured information was
entered into a normalized relational database, preserving
the original expert’s assessment of whether a finding was
"treatment-related" - whether the finding was considered
to be associated with the overall experimental intervention (which can include the test article, vehicle/formulation, route/procedure, handling, anesthesia/surgery etc.). To
overcome inconsistent terminology from different sources,
all verbatim findings were thoroughly mapped to a standardized set of preferred terms using ontologies specifically
developed as part of the consortium (Briggs et al. 2012).
These ontologies also captured hierarchical relationships,
allowing the system to intelligently aggregate and query
related findings, such as treating a finding in a “liver lobule”
as a “liver” finding, to create harmonized datasets for analysis (López-Massaguer et al. 2018).
The eTRANSAFE consortium was a successor project to
eTOX. It was similarly funded by the Innovative Medicines
Initiative (IMI) and focused on developing integrated databases and computational tools to support the translational
safety assessment of new drugs (Pognan et al. 2021; Sarntivijai et al. 1000). A key focus of the project was on data
sharing and integrating various data sources into harmonized repositories, utilizing standards like the SEND terminology. As one of its milestones, the consortium developed
the PretoxTM text-mining system to extract test articlerelated findings from unstructured preclinical toxicology
reports (Corvi et al. 2025).
While some initiatives like eTOX and eTRANSAFE
focussed on text-mining unstructured legacy reports,
other initiatives have been primarily centered on leveraging structured, standardized raw data. One of them was a
public-private collaboration (Carfagna et al. 2024), a joint
effort between BioCelerate and the U.S. Food and Drug
Administration’s (FDA) Center for Drug Evaluation and
Research (CDER). This collaboration developed an opensource platform to analyze and compare raw, subject-level
toxicology data from multiple studies. This system utilizes
the raw SEND data, thereby avoiding the manual curation
of information from static PDF reports. The methodology
involves normalizing numerical data against control groups
using Z-scores and assigning a unified “ToxScore” to microscopic findings based on their incidence and severity. These
scores are then aggregated to create visual toxicity profiles
for key organ systems, which are displayed on an interactive dashboard featuring tools like radar plots for high-level
comparison. This framework allows scientists to efficiently
compare toxicity profiles across different compounds, species, and study durations to better understand a compound’s
effects (Carfagna et al. 2024). While this approach capitalized on the richness of SEND, it did not address the absence
of expert interpretations- the crucial study-level conclusions on causality and adversity.
The SR-Domain concept was designed to fill this gap.
This novel data standard, proposed by Phil Drew from PDS
Consultants, during the lifetime of the eTRANSAFE consortium, is aligned with SEND v3.1, and provides a structured framework for capturing essential toxicological results
from study reports (Corvi et al. 2025). The SR-Domain data
are ideally stored together with the underlying study measurement raw data in the same SEND-based preclinical data
warehouse. This powerful link allows sponsors to retrieve
observations derived from expert opinion and to directly
compare them with (or differentiate them from) incidental
observations.
In contrast to the raw data, the SR-Domain template
does not contain subject-level data. It organizes study-wide
observations, such as test article-related effects and expert
conclusions, into distinct records qualified by group, sex,
and other experimental parameters. By utilizing the CDISC
SEND Controlled Terminology, it ensures consistency and
interoperability across different data systems. This approach
facilitates a direct, one-to-one transcription of vital information from the final report, creating a harmonized and readily
analyzable dataset. The implementation of the SR-Domain
represents a significant step towards the systematic structuring of non-clinical study data for improved data sharing and
analysis (Corvi et al. 2025). Due to its success even after
the eTRANSAFE consortium concluded, the SR-Domain
has been suggested as a potential extension to the SEND
standard, maintained by CDISC.
All these initiatives demonstrated the feasibility of data
integration but exposed persistent challenges. A comparative evaluation of these initiatives underscores their
complementary strengths and inherent limitations. Each
project addressed a different layer of the toxicology data
challenge—ranging from manual curation and ontology

development (eTOX), to semi-automated text extraction and
data integration (eTRANSAFE), to computational visualization of structured raw data (FDA/BioCelerate). However,
none of them achieved the synthesis of scalability, automation, interpretive depth, and terminological consistency
required for seamless, AI-ready toxicology data integration.
These limitations exposed a persistent gap between structured data availability and expert semantic understanding
— a gap that can now be bridged through a Large Language
Model (LLM)–supported automation integrated within the
SR-Domain framework.

In this paper, we aim to address the long-standing gap
between unstructured expert toxicological interpretation
and structured, machine-readable data by presenting a fully
integrated framework for the creation, harmonization, and
application of the Study Report (SR) domain. We describe
the conceptual foundations of the SR-Domain, outline its
alignment with SEND-controlled terminology, and introduce
a novel LLM-supported pipeline capable of extracting test
article-related findings from diverse toxicology reports with
high fidelity. Our work demonstrates how semantic understanding, automated document parsing, and ontology-aligned
harmonization can transform narrative study conclusions
into standardized datasets suitable for data integration, crossstudy querying, and AI/ML model development. Through
evaluation on 200 Roche preclinical toxicology reports, we
highlight the accuracy, robustness, and translational value of
this approach. Collectively, this publication establishes the
SR-Domain and its supporting LLM workflow as an enabling
foundation for data-centric toxicology, improved knowledge
sharing, and the future standardization of expert-derived conclusions in nonclinical safety assessment.
Methodology
SR-Domain concept and structure
Initial design
The original concept of the SR-Domain is to be designed
as a tabular dataset able to capture key study conclusions
from toxicity study reports (Corvi et al. 2025). Each record
in the Study Report dataset contains an observation which
is qualified by the group, sex, specimen and test to which
it relates. It can be further qualified by day, severity and
statistical significance. Study-wide Effect Levels are also
accommodated.
Each entry is defined by a comprehensive set of variables that characterize the finding. These variables include
identifiers for the study (STUDYID), as well as record
qualifiers specifying the group (SRGRP/SPGRPCD) and
sex (SRSEX) in which the finding was observed, whereby
the SR-Domain reports observations for male and female
subjects separately even when the observations applies
for both sexes. Key fields capture the domain of the finding (SRDOMAIN) and its specific specimen (SRSPEC).
The observation itself is characterized by its verbatim text
(SRORES, original result), the test short name (SRTSTCD)
or the finding description (SRFNDG), its manifestation
(SROBSV, e.g., Increase or Decrease), severity (SRSEV),
and statistical significance (SRSIGF). The field SRTRTEF
is intended to indicate the expert’s conclusion on whether
the finding is treatment-related (’Y’), not treatment-related
(’N’), or uncertain (’U’). The Comment field (SRCOMNT)
allows capturing additional free text. The domain also
accommodates study-wide effect levels, such as NOAEL
(No Observed Adverse Effect Level) or LOAEL (Lowest
Observed Adverse Effect Level), contained in the SRRISK
field. Wherever feasible, the SR-Domain concept utilizes
terms obtained from the CDISC SEND Controlled Terminology to ensure descriptions of findings are standardized
and consistent and compatible with measurement raw data
(Corvi et al. 2025).

needs

several new variables while retaining the core structure of
the original standard. Key modifications include the addition
of fields to document the administered test article, identified target organs of toxicity as defined by the Study Director, result modifiers, and the reversibility of any observed
effects. Furthermore, the internal data model incorporates

LLM workflow
Unlike traditional text-mining systems that rely on rulebased keyword extraction, our LLM-based pipeline performs semantic interpretation of toxicological statements.
It identifies causal relationships between compounds, doses,
and effects while retaining contextual qualifiers such as
time-dependence, reversibility, and adversity. This enables
the automated translation of narrative toxicology reports
into SR-Domain–compatible records that preserve expert
reasoning in a structured format. Importantly, the framework is model-agnostic and extensible, allowing future
fine-tuning with domain-specific corpora to continuously
improve accuracy (Fig. 2).

The length of these reports can vary from a few dozen pages
up to a few hundreds; in some cases, reports may also contain over a thousand pages. However, most of these pages
are associated with the appendix section that is irrelevant for
SR-Domain extraction. A Python-based solution leveraging
PyMuPDF (Fenniak et al. 2024) and PyPDF2 (Artife 2026)
libraries, was developed to extract the Table of Contents
(TOC). When the TOC was unavailable, the initial pages
were parsed to detect TOC-like structures using specific
trigger words such as "reference," "archiving," and "signature." These trigger words, appearing strictly as TOC section headers, reliably indicated appendix sections without
false positives. Empirical evaluation confirmed that appendices always started within the first 60 pages; thus, if TOC
detection failed, retaining the first 100 pages was conservatively sufficient, with no exceptions observed.
Parsing For our document parsing needs, we adapted and
implemented a multi-module pipeline inspired by olmOCR,
an open source tool (Poznanski et al. 2025). Its main advantage consists of using a visual language model, when compared to other PDF processing tools that rely on classic OCR
(optical character recognition). Thus this toolbox systematically processes documents to ensure high-fidelity content
extraction from a diverse range of sources, including those
with complex layouts containing text, tables, and mathematical formulas. After extracting the information into a
markdown format the section structure of the document is
reconstructed by identifying headers and sub-headers of the
sections. This process relies on classic NLP approaches. As
final output, a markdown with clearly defined sections is
obtained.
Identifying relevant sections Initially, entire reports were
input into LLMs for extraction, leading to poor results
due to irrelevant data inclusion. Consequently, preprocessing was refined to precisely identify document structures,
specifically targeting sections labeled “Summary," "Introduction”, “Discussion”, and “Conclusion”, where test
article-related findings typically appear. The initial parsing
output identified headers but lacked granularity in capturing hierarchical relationships. To address this, an algorithm
was developed using regular expressions to detect standard
hierarchical numbering schemes (e.g., “1.”, “1.1.”, etc.).
The approach allowed some tolerance for missing intermediate subsections (e.g., capturing “1.1” and then “1.3” while

occasionally missing “1.2”), prioritizing semantic accuracy
with a preference for false negatives over false positives.
Older reports, lacking hierarchical structures entirely, were
adequately handled by simpler parser outputs—identifying
only primary section headers. This simpler method was sufficient due to the limited relevance of reports over 30–40
years old. The final algorithm integrates regular expressionbased identification and parser-derived information, ensuring optimized extraction accuracy across diverse report
formats.
Extraction The extraction phase leverages LLMs to systematically extract test article-related findings from preprocessed documents according to a structured schema. The
model used for the generation was the OpenAI gpt−4.1
model (Openai 2025). The schema is a python file that relies
on class based coding, where each finding type is a separate class (e.g. MetaData) with its relevant properties (e.g.
ReportName, SubstanceID). This approach ensures data
consistency by enumerating expected outputs and enabling
precise validation through conversion to Python data structures. Distinct extraction pipelines were developed for
multi-phase studies, combination studies, and single-treatment studies, reflecting unique information presentations in
each report type. Metadata extracted from an internal database containing study titles and Roche compound numbers
can be explicitly integrated into LLM prompts extraction
accuracy. Context-loss issues were identified in LLMs,
especially with repetitive mentions of similar entities.
Attempts to address this through adjustments to repetition
penalties and temperature parameters were insufficient, primarily because repetition penalties target token repetition
over short distances. Repetitive entities appearing at intervals exceeding token limits rendered these adjustments ineffective. Consequently, a recursive extraction strategy was
adopted, partitioning documents into smaller segments. This
method substantially reduced instances where no data was
extracted—from 20 reports initially down to only 2—also
addressing token limitations by enabling separate extractions for each smaller segment.
Curation “Human-in-the-loop” analyzed the retrieved
findings and provided feedback to improve the extraction.
This step was facilitated by providing the sentences from
the original reports that were used by the model to identify the finding. A section name, and sentences around the
finding sentence were also provided to facilitate the comparison with the ground truth in the reports. The suggestions
for improvement were mainly of two types. The first type
focused on refining the LLM prompt in those cases where
the findings were not extracted correctly. To improve that,
besides the data curator, experts in their fields (e.g. toxicologists) were involved to formulate the goals of extractions
more clearly. In the second type, the improvements were
centered on going back to the preprocessing step. Here,
the objective was to verify that all the relevant report sections were captured correctly and not excluded, as well as to
ensure that the amount of information that is unrelated to the
extraction objectives is minimized.
SENDification Effective data harmonization post-extraction ensures consistent terminology across extracted findings. We employed the SEND, a CDISC standard providing
a comprehensive controlled vocabulary tailored to nonclinical study data. A semantic search-based retrieval pipeline
was implemented, utilizing vector embeddings precomputed with the abhinand/MedEmbed-small-v0.1 model.
The cosine similarity threshold of 0.85 was set empirically based on iterative evaluations. Terms extracted from
Laboratory Tests (LB), Microscopic Findings (MI), and
Organ Measurements (OM) were mapped to SEND terms
if exceeding this threshold; otherwise, fields were left blank
to avoid inaccuracies. Due to their complexity, Microscopic
findings required additional processing. The OpenAI textembedding-large-3 model (Openai 2024) retrieved the four
most semantically similar SEND terms for each query, and
GPT−4.1 generated standardized SEND-aligned terms from
these candidates. Human domain experts iteratively validated these generated terms, ensuring critical nuances were
preserved. A further harmonization step (SENDification)
ensured compliance with SEND standards.
SR-Domain file generation Extracted findings were
organized into individual study files to facilitate targeted
data access. Findings were categorized according to Clinical Chemistry (LB), Histopathology (MI), Macroscopic
(MA), Hematology (LB), Organ and Body Weight (OM
and BW respectively), Death (DD), Clinical Signs (CL),
NOAEL, LOAEL, MTD, and Target Organs of Toxicity
(TOT). The last four were reported under SRRISK. TOT is
a novel domain introduced by Roche domain experts due
to its unique relevance distinct from MI or OM findings.
Meta data that was relevant for various SR-Domain fields
was also extracted as a separate category. Each original data
record corresponds directly to a single record in the generated files. The structured dataset comprises detailed fields
aligned with SR-Domain standards, ensuring consistency,
accessibility, and utility for future research and regulatory
review. The pipeline can be found in the following github
repository.
Evaluation of the performances of the LLM-based
pipeline
To assess the performance of the LLM-based pipeline, we
evaluated its outputs against a manually curated reference
using precision, sensitivity, and specificity metrics.
1 3
Archives of Toxicology
● Precision was used to quantify the reliability of the extractions by measuring the proportion of correctly extracted information among all reported findings, reflecting the trustworthiness of the structured outputs.
● Sensitivity (recall) measured the ability of the pipeline
to correctly retrieve relevant information present in the
study reports, thereby quantifying the extent to which
true information was successfully captured.
● Specificity assessed the ability of the pipeline to correctly identify the absence of findings and avoid spurious extractions, providing a measure of how many true
negatives are correctly rejected.
● Accuracy was defined as the proportion of correct extraction (true positives and true negatives) across all
evaluated domains.
● The F1-score was reported as a complementary summary metric, capturing the harmonic mean of precision
and sensitivity.
Together, these metrics provide a comprehensive and biologically interpretable assessment of extraction accuracy,
balancing information recovery with error avoidance.
Detailed information on the performance of the different
metrix can be found in Annex 2.

Results
Evaluation of the performance of the LLM-data
extraction
LLMs are known to be prone to hallucinations, particularly
when asked to generate free-form narrative responses. To
ensure reliability in an otherwise regulatory-relevant context, our extraction pipeline was explicitly designed to minimize such errors by enforcing a strictly structured output
format aligned with our enhanced SR-Domain schema. This
approach constrains the LLM to produce predefined fields
with controlled vocabularies, which substantially improves
factual accuracy and limits generative drift. Within this
framework, we evaluated the performance of the extraction
system across 200 preclinical toxicology Roche reports.
Table 3 summarizes extraction performance across all
evaluated domains, with a primary focus on sensitivity and
specificity. Overall, the extraction pipeline demonstrated
consistently high sensitivity across parameters, indicating robust detection of relevant findings, while specificity
showed greater variability depending on parameter type and
domain.
For Clinical Chemistry and Hematology, sensitivity was generally high, ranging from 84−87%, with
most core parameters such as Adversity, ChangeType,
TimeDependence, and Sex consistently exceeding 85%
sensitivity. Specificity in these domains was also strong
for most parameters (91−99%), reflecting a low rate of
false-positive extractions. However, Route of Administration showed reduced sensitivity (72%) and specificity
(61−78%), and DoseObserved and SubstanceID exhibited
lower specificity, suggesting challenges in distinguishing
true negatives when these attributes were implicitly or
inconsistently reported.
Histopathology demonstrated the highest sensitivity
across all domains, with most parameters achieving sensitivities between 94−96%, underscoring the model’s ability
to reliably capture histopathological findings and associated
interpretive attributes. Specificity in this domain was near
perfect for several parameters (Adversity, ChangeType,
Reversibility, Severity, and TimeDependence), while
remaining lower for parameters such as Route of Administration, DoseObserved, and SubstanceID. This pattern
reflects the limited number of true-negative examples for
these attributes rather than reduced detection capability.
Organ Weight findings also showed strong performance,
with sensitivities predominantly above 95% and specificities between 89−98% for most parameters. High specificity
for Organ, ChangeType, Sex, and TimeDependence indicates effective suppression of false positives, while slightly
reduced specificity for SubstanceID and StudyType suggests residual ambiguity in these fields.

With this level of accuracy across key toxicological
domains, the generated SR-Domain outputs are robust
enough to support a range of scientific and decision-making workflows. The following section illustrates how these
structured data can be operationalized in practice.
Applications of structured SR-Domain data in
toxicology and drug safety
The structured nature of SR-Domain data enables a wide
range of practical applications that support toxicological
assessment, decision-making, and predictive modeling.
By transforming expert-derived study conclusions into
standardized, machine-readable records, the SR-Domain
provides an interpretable and queryable layer that complements raw SEND data and bridges chemistry, pathology,
and exposure information. In the following subsections, we
highlight several representative use cases that illustrate how
SR-Domain data can be leveraged for discovery program
support, reference compound identification, cross-modal
data integration (e.g. in vitro with in vivo data), and the
development of predictive AI/ML toxicology models.
Use case 1: identification of internal reference compounds
for assay development and validation
One of the immediate and impactful applications of structured SR-Domain data is the identification of positive
reference compounds for context-of-use (COU) assay development and validation, particularly through the systematic
characterization of Target Organs of Toxicity (TOT).
Because the SR-Domain encodes test article-related
findings in a standardized format, it enables rapid identification of compounds that show clear, test article–related
effects in specific organs or organ systems of interest. These
compounds can serve as reliable “positives” for developing or evaluating new approach methods (NAMs) aimed at
predicting the same liabilities, e.g., to identify positive reference compounds relevant for a “lung-on-a-chip” model.
SR-Domain data can be filtered broadly for “Lung” in the
Target Organs of Toxicity (TOT) column; alternatively, the
Finding Description (SRFNDG) and/or the Original Result
(SRORES) can be used to narrow these entries further
down to specific mode of toxicities (e.g. “alveolar epithelial
hyperplasia”).
The reliable identification of “negatives” needs a very
different approach: The absence of test article-related findings in the SR-Domain is not considered sufficient, since
annotations could be missing or incomplete. It is therefore
recommended to check the Microscopic Findings (MI)
domain of the corresponding SEND datasets to make sure
a) the tissue of interest was in fact examined and b) the
absence of microscopic findings was recorded accordingly
(“No abnormalities detected”) across all dose groups and
study durations, assuming appropriate exposure.
Combining SR-Domain–derived positives with MIderived negatives enables the creation of balanced, interpretable reference sets for assay calibration, threshold setting,
and performance evaluation. This structured approach
supports the establishment of quantitative context-of-use
criteria, which are essential for NAM development, assay
qualification, and translational relevance assessment.
Use case 2: target organ toxicity annotation dashboard
The automated extraction and standardization of test-article related findings into the SR-Domain provides a strong
foundation for predictive toxicology. While the LLMbased system addresses the primary bottleneck of digitizing
unstructured text into standardized formats, the downstream
utility of this data is best exemplified by the Target Organ
Toxicity Annotation Dashboard (TOAD). TOAD is an internally developed user interface that exposes aggregated toxicity data. By aggregating findings across variable study
designs, ranging from single dose pilot studies to repeat 
1 3
Archives of Toxicology
dose GLP protocols across multiple species, routes of
administration, TOAD constructs a composite safety profile
for each compound.
The system employs a rigorous ternary classification
scheme using Red, Green, and Gray colors to visualize target organ toxicity. This logic is critical for curating high
confidence training data for quantitative structure activity
relationship (QSAR) models. For predictive modeling to be
effective, the underlying in vivo weight-of-evidence must be
strictly categorized, distinguishing between confirmed testarticle related findings (‘Positive’), no test-article related
findings (‘Negative’), and the lack of substantial evidence
(‘Unknown’).
Leveraging SR-Domain data to identify ‘positive’
compounds
The primary value of the automated SR-Domain data
generation lies in its ability to centralize positive toxicity
signals. The SR-Domain acts as a high level container that
aggregates adverse findings derived from detailed finding
domains, including microscopic (MI), macroscopic (MA),
organ weights (OM), and clinical pathology (LB). TOAD
leverages this structured data to identify ‘Positive’ compounds without the need for manual review of the underlying raw data (Fig. 3).
To designate a compound as ‘Positive’ for a specific target organ, such as liver, TOAD queries the SR-Domain data
for test-article related findings called under different finding
domains (see Figs. 3 and 4). Because a single compound
may be tested in disparate studies, such as a 14-day rat study
and a 28-day dog study, TOAD aggregates these signals at
the compound level. If any study reports a finding related
to the test article in the target organ, the dashboard flags the
compound as positive. This approach ensures that the ‘Positive’ label in the QSAR training set is derived exclusively
from findings where a toxicological association was explicitly established by the study authors, thereby reducing noise
from incidental background findings.
Leveraging MI domain data to identify ‘negative’
compounds
While the SR-Domain data effectively captures true
‘Positive’ compounds, relying solely on the absence of SR
records is insufficient to confirm if a compound is truly
‘Negative’. In data science terms, the absence of a signal
in the SR-Domain is ambiguous. It could imply the target
organ is clean, representing a true ‘Negative’, or the target organ was never collected or examined, representing
missing data. To compile robust ‘Negative’ (Green) data
for predictive modeling, TOAD implements a secondary
verification step using raw microscopic (MI) domain data.
A compound is classified as ‘Negative’ for a specific target
organ only if two strict criteria are satisfied. First, the SRDomain data must contain no test-article related finding for
the organ in question. Second, the raw MI dataset is queried
to confirm that the organ was historically evaluated. TOAD
filters for records where the specimen name (MISPEC)
matches the target organ and validates that raw MI records
exist and all study animals have the ‘MISTRESC’ annotated
as ‘UNREMARKABLE’ or ‘NORMAL’.
This cross referencing is essential for statistical integrity. If
MISPEC entries exist, confirming the pathologist examined
the tissue, but no corresponding SR entries exist, the endpoint
is confirmed as a true ‘Negative’. Conversely, if the organ

Use case 3: strategy support
The SR-Domain facilitates retrospective analyses of historical
study designs, providing data-driven guidance for future study
planning. A critical challenge in designing Dose Range Finding (DRF) studies is estimating the maximum tolerable dose
(MTD) while optimizing resource utilization and ensuring animal welfare.
In a recent application, the SR-Domain was utilized to support the strategy for high-dose selection by analyzing repeatdose toxicology studies conducted over the past decade. The
objective was to investigate the prevalence and tolerability of
high doses in non-rodent species to derive safe upper-limit estimates for upcoming programs.
By querying the structured dataset, we extracted dose distributions across 81 GLP and various Non-GLP studies. A key
advantage of the SR-Domain integration was the ability to
layer study findings with comprehensive metadata, allowing us
to segregate data not only by species (rodent vs. non-rodent)
and administration route, but crucially by molecule modality—
distinguishing between Small Molecules, Large Molecules,
and RNA-based therapeutics. The analysis revealed that while
the majority of non-rodent oral doses remained below 600 mg/
kg, specific instances of high-dose tolerance were identifiable.
Specifically, the query isolated five studies utilizing dose ≥
1000 mg/kg/day in minipig and cynomolgus monkey, including one study reaching 2000 mg/kg/day (Fig. 5).
Because the SR-Domain links these parametric data
points back to the specific study context, toxicologists could
immediately identify the programs and retrieve the associated safety profiles. This capability allows teams to contextualize high-dose decisions—understanding whether they
were driven by good tolerability or specific pharmacokinetic requirements—rather than relying on theoretical maximums. Consequently, this supports more precise estimation
of safety margins and resource allocation for future DRF
studies, preventing unnecessary compound consumption
and refining dose selection based on historical evidence.

On the technical side, first a vector search based on cosine
similarity between query project finding and the database is performed to obtain similar findings. For example, the top 20 most
similar findings about other projects can be extracted. Then an
LLM evaluates the organ information from the metadata to
discard any irrelevant findings. In the next step, a ranking of
findings from most similar to least similar is performed. Here,
both metadata and the finding itself are considered. The lowest
ranked findings can be discarded and the remaining ones can be
harmonized. For instance, “tubular degeneration/regeneration”
and “cortical tubular degeneration/regeneration” are essentially
the same finding with different detail levels and can be grouped
together under the “tubular degeneration/regeneration” label.

molecules that are similar to the query finding. This can result
as a starting point for a more in depth comparison and analysis
among them. The comparison can be done based on the organ

Use case 5: enabling AI/ML model
The SR-Domain findings can for simpler analysis. For instance,
it allows us to assess how different drug modalities influence
the target organ of toxicity (Fig. 7).
Moreover, it is also possible to zoom into either modality
to analyze specific trends in more detail. For small molecules,
one can examine intersections of target-organ toxicities (Fig.
8a and b) as well as pairwise toxicity correlations, both for cooccurring toxicities within the same substance (Fig. 9a) and for
toxicity correlations between tested species (Fig. 9b).
The SR-Domain analysis and the findings themselves thus
provide a standardized and interpretable foundation for developing AI and machine learning (AI/ML) models aimed at predicting in vivo toxicity outcomes from chemical or multimodal
input data. A long-standing barrier to predictive toxicology
has been the scarcity of high-quality, expert-annotated labels
that distinguish test article-related findings from incidental
background changes. Because the SR-Domain encodes these
expert conclusions—along with adversity, dose-dependency,
time-dependency, and target organ information—in a machinereadable and harmonized format, it offers precisely the kind of
curated labels required for robust model training.
Using SR-Domain data, investigators can construct welldefined positive and negative training sets for a wide range of
in vivo endpoints. For example, compounds with test articlerelated findings in a specific organ (e.g., liver, kidney, heart)
can serve as “positives,” while compounds with clean findings
in the corresponding SEND MI or OM domains can serve as
“negatives.” This approach supports the development of endpoint-specific classifiers, multi-organ toxicity predictors, and
multitask neural models. Importantly, the SR-Domain facilitates aggregation of findings across studies, species, and dose
levels, thereby enabling training sets with sufficient sample
sizes—an essential requirement noted prior in silico toxicology research.
Beyond enabling classic classification models, SR-Domain
data also support more advanced strategies, such as clustering
of toxicity profiles, learning structure–toxicity relationships,
and integrating in vivo outcomes with in vitro NAM data, transcriptomics, or other omics layers. Because each SR-Domain
record is grounded in a toxicologist’s interpretation, the resulting labels offer a level of biological relevance and contextual
nuance that is difficult to obtain from raw numeric data alone.
Overall, the SR-Domain transforms legacy study report text
into a high-quality supervisory signal, allowing AI/ML models to be trained on standardized, interpretable, and scalable
datasets. This opens the door to more reliable and transparent
predictive toxicology tools that can complement human assessment and enhance translational safety evaluation.
Discussion
This work demonstrates that a structured, semantic, and
SEND-aligned representation of study-level toxicology
conclusions can be generated at scale from unstructured

PDF study reports using a LLM–supported extraction pipeline. Our findings show that, when tightly constrained by an
SR-Domain tailored JSON schema and combined with preprocessing, section identification, and harmonization steps,
LLMs can overcome many of the challenges traditionally
associated with automated extraction of toxicology information as well as manual extraction.
Former initiatives such as eTOX, eTRANSAFE, and the
FDA/BioCelerate collaborations highlighted both the promise and limitations of using legacy study reports or structured SEND data for modern, data-centric toxicology. These

projects addressed isolated aspects of the problem - manual

the combination of scalability, interpretive depth, consistency, and automation required for large-scale data integration. The SR-Domain and LLM workflow presented here
directly address this gap by merging expert-level semantic
interpretation with a SEND-compatible, machine-readable
structure.
Our performance evaluation across 200 Roche preclinical
reports demonstrated consistently high sensitivity across all
evaluated domains, indicating robust detection of relevant
toxicological findings. Specificity was also strong for most
parameters, particularly those with standardized terminology, while showing greater variability for attributes that are
implicitly reported or inconsistently documented. Domains
such as Histopathology, Organ Weight, and NOAEL-related
endpoints exhibited the most robust performance, with sensitivities typically exceeding 95% and high specificity for
key interpretive fields.

Several methodological innovations contributed to
the observed performance. First, the preprocessing steps
(appendix trimming, TOC detection, and structured markdown conversion) removed large volumes of irrelevant content and ensured that models focused on sections where test
article-related findings reside. Second, the recursive extraction strategy minimized context loss - an inherent limitation
of LLMs when handling long documents - and substantially

applying vector-based semantic similarity followed by
SENDification ensured terminological consistency, a critical requirement for modelling purposes.
In addition to demonstrating extraction accuracy, we
illustrated several high-value applications enabled by SRDomain datasets. These include: (i) the identification of
internal reference compounds and the construction of positive/negative sets for NAM development and assay validation; (ii) portfolio-wide toxicology queries integrating
chemistry, mechanisms, and in vivo outcomes; (iii) crossmodal linking of in vivo findings with in vitro assay results;
and (iv) development of AI/ML models using high-quality
test article-related labels derived from toxicologist interpretation. These use cases highlight the versatility of the SRDomain as a unifying layer that connects diverse data and
supports translational and predictive toxicology efforts.
Despite the strong performance of the LLM extraction pipeline, there are areas for future improvement.
Heterogeneity in study report styles—especially in older
documents—continues to introduce ambiguity that may
challenge extraction precision. Further LLM fine-tuning on
domain-specific corpora, possibly combined with reinforcement learning from human feedback, may reduce variability in such cases. Additionally, automated SENDification of
novel or rarely reported terms remains a partially supervised
process, and continued expert oversight is recommended to
maintain semantic integrity. Finally, broad regulatory adoption will require further validation, including reproducibility assessments, provenance tracking, and community
alignment around SR-Domain field definitions.
The original SR-Domain concept would benefit from
further significant refinement due to two main problems:
first, the conflation of concepts, where the current SRDomain mixes data structures related to the overall study
(e.g. NOAEL) with those specific to groups/animals (e.g.,
creatinine increase in the high dose group), unlike the general SEND implementation which limits a domain to a single data structure; and second, the presence of competing
variable definitions, where several variables have multiple
assigned meanings, complicating data collection and usage.
Resolving these two primary concerns is essential, as they
currently impede effective data storage, querying, and the
training of LLMs.
Overall, the SR-Domain combined with a constrained
LLM pipeline provides a scalable, interpretable, and regulatory-aligned solution for structuring toxicological knowledge previously locked within narrative PDF reports. This
framework supports modern, data-driven toxicology and
creates a foundation for cross-study integration, translational insight generation, and next-generation predictive
modeling.
Conclusion
In this study, we introduce a comprehensive framework
for converting toxicology study reports into structured,
machine-readable SR-Domain datasets using an LLMsupported extraction pipeline. By aligning extracted findings with SEND-controlled terminology and enforcing
schema-based structure, we demonstrate that expert-level
interpretations such as test article relatedness, adversity,
dose-dependence, and target organ identification, can be
captured reliably and at scale. Evaluation across 200 preclinical reports confirmed high extraction accuracy, supporting the robustness of this approach (Table 4).
The resulting SR-Domain datasets unlock a wide array of
applications, including reference compound identification,
portfolio-level toxicology queries, cross-modal data integration, and AI/ML model development. These capabilities
transform unstructured study narratives into a unified, queryable, and analysis-ready knowledge layer that enhances
internal decision-making and supports translational safety
strategies (Table 8).
Looking forward, adoption of the SR-Domain as a standardized extension to SEND—and continued refinement of
the LLM pipeline through domain-specific fine-tuning and
harmonization—may further strengthen its regulatory utility and enable broader community use (Corvi et al. 2025).
By bridging the gap between narrative expert interpretation
and structured digital formats, the SR-Domain represents a
significant step toward data-centric toxicology and the modernization of nonclinical safety assessment.
