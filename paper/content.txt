Automated Ontology Annotation of Phenotypic Descriptions using Large Language Models (LLMs)

Abstract
1-Introduction
Biomedical studies worldwide generate immense volumes of phenotypic observations, creating unprecedented opportunities for predictive modelling. Yet, in toxicology, these critical data points often remain difficult to integrate into modern machine learning (ML) frameworks because key phenotypic findings are primarily recorded as unstructured free text in study reports [16]. To enable computational analysis, these text-based descriptions must first be normalised to standardised controlled vocabularies. Ontologies address this need by providing structured, machine-readable representations of biomedical knowledge, where phenotypic concepts are defined through explicit semantic and hierarchical relationships [23]. However, mapping unstructured text to these ontology terms has traditionally relied on manual expert curation [27]. This painstakingly slow and resource-intensive process is fundamentally unscalable given the continuous and rapid expansion of biomedical datasets.
A prominent example of this challenge in toxicology is the Toxicity Values Database (ToxValDB) [1], a centralised resource developed by the U.S. EPA Center for Computational Toxicology and Exposure to curate and standardise human health-relevant toxicity information. ToxValDB integrates more than 100,000 in vivo experimental results from diverse regulatory and research sources, harmonising experimental metadata and quantitative toxicity metrics into standardised Dose-Response Summary Values (DRSVs), including Points of Departure (PODs) such as NOAEL and LOAEL. However, despite being a key component of each record, the toxicological effect describing the observed experimental outcome remains recorded as unstructured free text. Because terminology, reporting conventions, and regulatory requirements vary across contributing sources, this field is difficult to standardise and creates a major bottleneck for predictive toxicology. Modern ML workflows require structured target variables linking quantitative dose information to well-defined phenotypic outcomes, particularly when integrating New Approach Methodologies (NAMs) such as Cell Painting profiles for compound activity and hazard prediction [21]. Recent LLM-curated toxicity resources such as UniTox have further demonstrated the value of transforming regulatory free text into structured toxicity labels for downstream molecular prediction tasks [17]. However, such resources typically provide drug-level or organ-level toxicity ratings, whereas ToxValDB requires effect-level normalisation to biologically specific ontology terms. Mapping these free-text effects to standardised ontology terms is therefore essential for using ToxValDB in predictive modelling and distinguishing compounds associated with adverse phenotypes from those with a documented absence of toxicity at specific dose levels.
To address this gap, we developed a large language model (LLM)-driven framework for converting unstructured biomedical observations into machine-readable ontology annotations, with the pipeline and study workflow summarised in Figure 1. This framework combines embedding-based Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [3] with ontology-aware validation to map legacy free-text records to established hierarchical vocabularies. Toxicological effect descriptions and ontology terms are first embedded into a shared vector space, enabling candidate ontology terms to be identified using a two-stage retrieve-and-rerank strategy. An LLM then validates the retrieved candidates by considering their definitions and positions within the target ontology hierarchy using a highly structured prompt. This ontology-agnostic design allows the same workflow to be adapted to different controlled vocabularies with minimal modification. We demonstrate the framework on the unstructured toxicological effect field in ToxValDB, mapping more than 16,000 unique effect descriptions to the Mammalian Phenotype (MP) ontology [2] with an estimated >100-fold speedup relative to manual curation. Accuracy was assessed using a representative subset manually annotated by domain experts. LLM-generated mappings showed high similarity to the human consensus reference, with human-LLM overlap comparable to, and often higher than, expert-expert overlap. Together with the high inter-model overlap observed across LLM backbones, these results support the framework as a scalable and reproducible approach for standardising complex biomedical free text.

2-Methods
2.1 Text-to-Ontology Mapping Framework
Our framework builds on recent advances in Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [3] and large language models (LLMs) for scientific literature synthesis [4] and biomedical text normalisation [5,6]. Previous phenotype concept recognition systems, such as PhenoTagger, have shown that hybrid dictionary and machine learning approaches can improve recognition of ontology concepts in biomedical text [18]. For example, methods like SPIRES have leveraged zero-shot learning in LLMs to populate schemas with ontology-grounded concepts [25], while recent workflows have integrated semantic retrieval, LLM-based refinement, and curator validation for mapping toxicological phenotypes to pathways and gene sets [26]. Whereas many existing approaches focus on clinical terminology normalisation or mention-level phenotype recognition, our implementation uses the structured hierarchies of biological ontologies as the knowledge base for retrieval and validation. Because biological ontologies can contain thousands of interconnected terms, exhaustive LLM-based evaluation of each potential candidate mapping is difficult to scale. A purely generative top-down traversal would require many sequential LLM calls, particularly for shallow or densely branching ontologies where hierarchical pruning provides limited reduction of the search space. To avoid this, our system uses a two-stage retrieve-and-rerank normalisation strategy that first reduces the search space to a compact set of plausible candidate terms for downstream LLM validation (Figure 1B).
This process begins by transforming both input descriptions and ontology term labels into dense vector embeddings using large, general-purpose embedding models rather than smaller domain-specific encoders [7]. These models provide broad semantic coverage, allowing the system to capture the biological meaning of non-standard or legacy phrasing. At this initial retrieval stage, ontology terms are represented by their labels alone, without definitions or hierarchical context, to preserve comparability with the short phrase-like input descriptions. Cosine similarity between the resulting vectors is then used in a bi-encoder retrieval step to identify the top K1 candidate ontology terms exceeding a predefined threshold α.
The initial candidate set is then refined through reranking. Although dense bi-encoder retrieval is effective for broad semantic matching, its independent encoding of input text and ontology terms may miss fine-grained semantic distinctions. Retrieved candidates are therefore passed to a specialised cross-encoder reranker, which jointly encodes each toxicological description–candidate term pair for more detailed comparison [8]. The reranker retains the top K2 candidates whose relevance scores exceed a second threshold ε.
Finally, the retained candidates are evaluated through an LLM-based validation step. The LLM considers both the definitions of the candidate ontology terms and their positions within the target ontology hierarchy, allowing it to distinguish between semantically similar but biologically distinct candidates. For each candidate, the model generates a structured explanation to support its mapping decision. The system then applies an iterative hierarchy-aware validation strategy. If the LLM rejects a candidate term, its parent terms are passed to the reranker for subsequent evaluation, allowing broader terms to be selected. Conversely, when a candidate is accepted, the annotation is propagated to its ancestor terms, and its immediate child terms trigger reranker evaluation for the next iteration. This bidirectional routing preserves broad biological validity while progressively refining mappings towards the most specific ontology level supported by the source text.
2.2 Target Ontology
The target ontology must represent the biological concepts in the source text at an appropriate level of specificity. For datasets like ToxValDB, which records phenotypic outcomes from mammalian in vivo studies, the ontology requires broad coverage and robust hierarchical relationships to support both general and specific annotations. We therefore treated ontology selection as a dataset-specific step, prioritising vocabularies whose terms, definitions, and graph structure best captured the corpus.
A recurring challenge when using established ontologies is that term metadata can be incomplete or inconsistently informative, limiting both embedding-based retrieval and downstream LLM validation. To address this, we implemented an auxiliary definition-generation step (see Supplementary Figure 1). When a term lacked a formal definition, an LLM generated one using the term label and its semantic and hierarchical context within the ontology graph. When an existing definition was too brief or incomplete, the LLM generated an auxiliary definition.
2.3 Case Study: ToxValDB Processing
We applied the pipeline to ToxValDB 9.7.0, focusing on the toxicological effect field. After evaluating candidate knowledge bases for biological relevance and coverage, we selected the Mammalian Phenotype Ontology (MP) as the target ontology (see Supplementary Table 1), given its broad coverage of mammalian phenotypic outcomes and hierarchical structure.
Before mapping to the MP ontology, we applied a normalisation protocol to isolate biological effects from experimental context. Descriptions containing multiple effects were split into individual records. Rule-based filters then removed procedural terminology, quantitative metrics, and sex or species modifiers, as these attributes are retained as structured metadata elsewhere. Finally, an LLM-based filtering step removed any remaining non-biological phrases, ensuring only meaningful effect descriptions entered the mapping stage.
2.4 Retrieval and Reranking Optimisation
Semantic mapping to the MP ontology required hyperparameter tuning of the retrieval, reranking, and LLM-validation stages. Because jointly optimising these components would create a large and computationally expensive search space, we first decoupled optimisation of retrieval and reranking from downstream LLM validation.
To support this process, we manually annotated an internal test set of 50 toxicological effects selected to maximise semantic diversity. Standardised effect embeddings were projected into a two-dimensional Principal Component Analysis (PCA) space and clustered using K-means, with the phrase closest to each centroid selected. Although this 2D projection captured only a modest fraction of the high-dimensional embedding variance, it served effectively as a diversity-oriented sampling strategy [9,10]. To prevent evaluation leakage, this internal test set did not overlap with the final external human-annotated test set.
Using this internal test set, we evaluated zero-shot retrieval performance by benchmarking four top-performing embedding models from the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB) leaderboard [11] (see Supplementary Table 2). We restricted candidate reranking evaluation to the Qwen3-Reranker family [8], selected for its strong reported performance.
We evaluated recall against context burden with a grid search over retrieval and reranking hyperparameters (Supplementary Table 3, Supplementary Figure 2). Enriching reranker inputs with ontology definitions and hierarchical context yielded no statistically significant recall gain (see Supplementary Figure 3). Because downstream LLM validation would filter biologically unsupported candidates, we prioritized recall and selected the simplest high-performing configuration.
The final setup combined Qwen3-Embedding-8B with Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B, achieving a recall of 0.95 with a context burden of 20 candidates. Larger models and expanded candidate sets produced marginal recall gains that did not justify their increased computational costs. This efficient configuration was adopted for the final pipeline to generate a high-quality candidate pool for LLM validation. Finally, toxicological effect descriptions were transformed into dense vector embeddings using this selected model, providing the input representation for semantic retrieval against the embedded MP ontology terms (see Supplementary Figure 1).
2.5 Test Set Design
To evaluate pipeline performance against expert human annotation, we constructed a semantically diverse test set of 50 toxicological phrases using the identical PCA and K-means clustering strategy described previously. For each test set effect, we generated candidate ontology terms using the optimised RAG system. While the full-dataset pipeline evaluated 20 candidates per effect, we restricted this human-comparison experiment to the top 10 candidates to reduce manual annotation burden. To ensure a symmetric comparison, both the LLMs and a panel of independent toxicology experts evaluated this identical candidate pool. To collect the manual annotations, we distributed a structured form to the expert panel (see Supplementary Figure 4,5 and 6). For each toxicological phrase, experts were asked to review the pool ontology terms and select all that constituted an accurate biological match. The humans were given a set of instructions that mirrored the prompt given to LLMs (see Supplementary Material S1). The human consensus reference was then defined as the ontology terms selected by at least two of these experts, excluding those who deemed the phrase uninterpretable or outside their expertise. LLM-generated mappings were subsequently evaluated against this baseline. Unless otherwise specified, both human and LLM annotation sets were expanded to include ontology ancestor terms before computing evaluation metrics (see Supplementary Material S2).
2.6 Hardware Requirements
We evaluated a panel of nine LLMs spanning diverse architectures (including dense and mixture-of-experts), generations, and parameter scales (see Supplementary Table 4). Common inference parameters, such as temperature, context limits, and reasoning settings, are detailed in the Supplementary Material S3.
We emphasized models deployable on a single 80 GB GPU to reduce hardware requirements, lower operational costs, and improve reproducibility in high-performance computing environments. Larger multi-GPU models were retained as high-capacity comparators to determine if increased computational cost yielded meaningful annotation improvements.
Open-weight models were served locally via vLLM [12] through an OpenAI-compatible API, making inference costs dependent on GPU allocation and runtime rather than token charges. The pipeline also supports external APIs for closed-source models. Alongside the LLM server, the RAG pipeline required one additional GPU for candidate embedding and reranking.
2.7 Prompt Design 
Because LLM reliability in complex data harmonisation depends on structured prompt engineering, we employed specialised prompts across all pipeline stages. Sequential preprocessing prompts standardised toxicological descriptions by assessing biological relevance, separating distinct concepts, removing metadata, and harmonising terminology. Additional prompts generated auxiliary definitions for incomplete ontology terms, guided cross-encoder reranking, and supported downstream LLM validation in both batch and single-term modes (see Supplementary Material S4).




















3-Results
3.1 LLM annotations align with human experts and form stable annotator profiles

Using the external human-annotated test set, we assessed whether LLM-generated ontology annotations showed overlap with expert judgement comparable to the overlap observed among human annotators, and whether LLMs formed stable annotation profiles within the broader human annotation landscape.

Human annotations showed substantial phrase-dependent differences, confirming that mapping toxicological free text to MP terms is not always a uniquely defined task. In many cases, different experts selected related but non-identical ontology terms, reflecting differences in biological interpretation, preferred ontology granularity, or the level of specificity considered supported by the source text. When comparing per-phrase human-human and LLM-LLM Jaccard similarity, we observed a significant positive correlation, indicating that toxicological effects with higher agreement among experts also tended to show higher agreement among LLMs (Figure 2A). This suggests that both humans and LLMs are sensitive to a shared underlying axis of annotation ambiguity, rather than producing unrelated disagreement patterns.

Across comparison groups, LLM-LLM Jaccard similarity was consistently higher than human-human similarity, indicating that different model backbones tended to converge on similar ontology mappings when given the same candidate terms and validation framework. Human-LLM Jaccard similarity was also comparable to, and often higher than, human-human similarity, indicating that LLM annotations were at least as aligned with expert judgement as annotations produced by individual experts were with one another (Figure 2B).

We then asked whether these agreement patterns translated into stable annotator-level profiles. Hierarchical clustering based on pairwise annotation overlap revealed that LLMs generally formed a compact group, indicating that different model backbones produced similar ontology annotation profiles under the same mapping framework (Figure 2C). Models from the same provider also tended to cluster together, suggesting that shared model architecture or training lineage may contribute to subtle but consistent annotation preferences. By contrast, human experts showed greater dispersion across the dendrogram. Some experts clustered close to the LLM group, suggesting similar interpretation and term-selection strategies, whereas others separated more strongly, indicating distinct annotation profiles.

These annotator-level profiles were also reflected in ontology-term usage. Human experts varied substantially in the mean number of ancestor-expanded ontology terms and in the mean per-effect maximum ontology depth reached by their annotations (Figure 2D). Some experts tended to select fewer and broader terms, whereas others produced more expansive annotations reaching deeper levels of the ontology. In comparison, LLMs showed a more consistent term-usage profile, generally producing a similar number of ancestor-expanded terms and reaching comparable ontology depths across model families.

Overall, these results suggest that disagreement in this annotation task is largely driven by the biological and semantic ambiguity of the toxicological descriptions themselves, together with systematic annotator-specific preferences in ontology granularity and coverage, rather than by inconsistent LLM behaviour. The correspondence between human and LLM agreement across phrases, the high inter-model overlap, and the coherent LLM annotation profile support the framework as a reproducible approach for ontology-based standardisation of complex biomedical free text.

3.2 Ontology-aware batch validation improves mapping performance

We evaluated how model configuration and LLM backbone influenced ontology-mapping performance using the human consensus reference. We first assessed three experimental parameters: inclusion of ontology definitions, candidate-evaluation mode, and the number of independent LLM consensus runs (1, 3, 5, or 10 generations, where ontology terms selected in at least 50% of the runs were retained). Candidate-evaluation mode had the clearest effect on performance (Figure 3). Batched evaluation, in which the LLM assessed the full candidate pool jointly, improved agreement with the human consensus reference compared with single-term evaluation, where each candidate was assessed independently. This suggests that access to the full candidate pool helps the LLM compare closely related ontology terms and select an appropriate level of specificity. Including ontology definitions produced a smaller benefit, likely because definitions provided additional context when candidate labels alone were ambiguous. In contrast, increasing the number of consensus runs did not substantially improve performance, indicating that a single structured validation run was sufficient under the selected prompt design. The most efficient configuration therefore combined batched evaluation, ontology definitions, and a single LLM consensus run.


Figure 3. Impact of LLM configuration parameters on ontology-mapping performance. F1 score against the human consensus reference was compared across ontology-definition settings, candidate-evaluation modes, and consensus-run settings using the external human-annotated test set.

We then compared performance across LLM backbones using this selected configuration. Overall, models showed broadly similar performance, indicating that the pipeline is not dependent on a single model family (Figure 4). Larger and newer models tended to perform slightly better, but performance was not determined solely by model scale. Several smaller or more efficient models achieved competitive results. Model families showed modest differences in the balance between precision, recall, and ontology overlap, but most maintained a similar overall F1 range. Similarly to the overall performance, the average number of generated tokens did not vary substantially across backbones, with OpenAI models generating the highest number of tokens on average. For the final large-scale annotation pipeline, we selected Qwen3.6-35B-A3B as the validation LLM because it provided the best performance (F1 Score) among models that fit on a single 80 GB GPU.

3.3 The pipeline scales to full ToxValDB annotation with high coverage
To evaluate real-world scalability, we deployed the selected framework across the full ToxValDB in vivo toxicological effect corpus. The system was hosted on a two-GPU node, with one 80 GB GPU serving the FP8-quantised validation LLM (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B) and a second GPU handling the bi-encoder and cross-encoder models of the RAG system. We used the FP8-quantised model after confirming that quantisation had a negligible impact on validation accuracy compared with the unquantised model (see Supplementary Figure 7). By parallelising requests, the pipeline processed approximately 17,000 unique effect descriptions in under three hours. Given that manual curation by domain experts proceeds at approximately 50 descriptions per hour (requiring an estimated 340 hours of expert labor for the full corpus), this automated execution represents a >100-fold speedup, supporting the framework's suitability for large-scale database standardisation.

The pipeline mapped 97.8% (16,356) of unique toxicological effect descriptions to at least one Mammalian Phenotype (MP) term, leaving only 2.2% (367) unmapped. This high coverage indicates that most historical ToxValDB effects can be represented using existing MP phenotype terms. The iterative hierarchy-aware validation step further improved coverage beyond the initial retrieval stage. Although semantic retrieval (RAG) accounted for 86.3% (157,255) of all accepted term instances, hierarchical routing recovered the remaining 13.7% (24,989). This shows that evaluating parent and child terms during validation recovered biologically valid annotations that were not captured among the initial RAG candidates.

The distribution of the maximum ontology depth assigned per toxicological effect (Figure 5A) shows that the mappings reached deep levels of the ontology hierarchy, indicating that the framework successfully resolved descriptions to specific phenotypic terms rather than broad, top-level concepts. Specifically, only a minor fraction of the effects were assigned shallow terms (depth 1 or 2), whereas the vast majority of annotations reached deeper levels (depths 4 to 6), peaking at depth 5. This distribution demonstrates that the LLM-driven validation step retains biologically specific granular annotations when supported by the source text.

We also analyzed the distribution of mapped effects across MP level 2 terms to identify patterns of target organ toxicity and systemic disruption (Figure 5B). This analysis revealed three key biological insights. First, we observed a clear primacy of systemic disruption over localized structural damage, as functional metabolic and homeostatic phenotypes vastly exceeded structural somatic domains. Second, we noted a high sensitivity of regulatory networks within organ-specific toxicity; the mapping was heavily dominated by complex, multi-organ signaling and regulatory systems rather than simpler excretion or ventilation organs. Third, the mapping coverage highlights clear assay-driven data limitations in historical toxicology reporting, as sensory phenotypes and developmental outcomes were the least frequently mapped. This reflects the fact that standard regulatory in vivo toxicology assays are primarily designed to monitor systemic and target-organ pathology rather than sensory or specialized developmental endpoints.

Finally, we assessed whether LLM-assigned ontology depth reflected historical differences in toxicological reporting practices across study publication years (Figure 5C). We observe that studies before ~1960 show a lower mean maximum depth, indicating that earlier records tend to support less specific phenotype annotations. This temporal pattern suggests that the pipeline successfully adjusted annotation granularity according to the specificity of the source text, reflecting the fact that older toxicity studies were often reported under less standardised and less stringent documentation practices.

4-Discussion
4.1 Framework Performance And Annotator Consensus
The integration of legacy toxicological data into modern machine learning workflows remains severely bottlenecked by the unstructured free text of historical study reports. Recent LLM-supported SR-Domain workflows have addressed the complementary upstream task of extracting expert toxicological interpretations from full preclinical study reports into SEND-aligned structured records [16]. Other initiatives have successfully combined semantic similarity matching, LLM-based filtering, and expert curation to map toxicological Key Events in Adverse Outcome Pathways (AOPs) to ontology and pathway terms [26]. By contrast, the unique strength of the LLM-driven pipeline presented in this study lies in its active exploitation of the ontology's hierarchical structure during retrieval and validation. This hierarchy-aware validation approach enables automated downstream normalisation that maps already curated toxicological effect descriptions to biologically expressive Mammalian Phenotype annotations with an estimated 100-fold speedup compared with manual curation.
A central finding of this study is the strong alignment observed across annotator groups, with human-human overlap consistently lower than both LLM-LLM and human-LLM overlap. The higher Jaccard similarity observed among different LLM backbones suggests that language models converge on a stable and reproducible annotation profile when operating under a unified reasoning framework. Notably, the finding that human-LLM agreement surpassed human-human agreement indicates that the automated framework can approximate the operational expert consensus defined in this study, aligning with individual specialist judgements at least as well as, and in some cases better than, specialists align with each other.
This interpretation should be considered in the context of the limited test-set size and the construction of the human reference. Expert-derived reference standards are often pragmatic aggregations of annotator judgements rather than perfect gold standards, and should be interpreted alongside inter-annotator agreement [24]. Accordingly, the human consensus reference used here reflects expert agreement within a shared candidate pool rather than an absolute ground truth. Nevertheless, the observed agreement patterns support the view that much of the annotation divergence arises from the semantic ambiguity of the biological source text, rather than from erratic model behaviour. Together, these findings position the LLM framework as a reproducible approach for reducing individual annotator-specific variation in ontology-based standardisation and for generating structured phenotype labels suitable for downstream computational toxicology.
4.2 Limitations And Future Directions
A primary limitation of the current framework is its focus on short, isolated phenotypic descriptions. This design reflects the nature of the ToxValDB dataset, where the effect descriptions had already been manually extracted from primary sources by curators. Consequently, the pipeline is not designed to extract and map observations directly from full-length scientific articles or toxicology study reports. Domain-specific systems such as PretoxTM show that extracting treatment-related findings from preclinical toxicology reports is feasible, but also that report-level extraction requires dedicated document parsing, section detection, entity normalisation, relation extraction, and expert validation [19]. Extending our framework to complete manuscripts will therefore require more advanced information extraction strategies. For example, flowchart-guided approaches such as GOFlowLLM [13] could improve extraction of specific biological outcomes from complex text, while recent visual retrieval-augmented generation models such as PixelRAG [14] may facilitate processing of full scientific documents by operating directly on page images, preserving layout and tabular information. Additionally, advanced document parsing systems like Mistral OCR 4 [15] can complement these efforts by translating complex layouts, multi-column text, and mathematical notations from raw documents into structured, citation-ready block primitives.
A separate limitation arises from the candidate-based annotation design. Because both human experts and LLMs evaluated the same retrieved candidates, the comparison provides a controlled assessment of ontology-term validation, but does not fully measure cases in which the most appropriate ontology term was absent from the candidate pool. Similarly, ancestor expansion provides a biologically meaningful way to capture semantic overlap between related ontology terms, but may partially obscure differences in annotation specificity by increasing agreement between broad and narrow mappings. This trade-off is consistent with broader work on semantic similarity in biomedical ontologies, where graph-based comparison enables biologically meaningful relaxed matching but remains sensitive to ontology depth, density, and annotation specificity [20]. Finally, the use of LLM-generated auxiliary ontology definitions improved coverage for sparsely defined terms, but these generated definitions may introduce model-specific bias or unsupported assumptions. Future implementations should therefore version, audit, and, where possible, manually review auxiliary definitions, especially for high-impact or frequently selected ontology terms.
Looking forward, several avenues exist to expand the utility and biological impact of this framework. A critical next step is to utilise these newly standardised toxicological labels as structured target variables in predictive toxicology, for example, to evaluate high-throughput phenotypic screening assays such as Cell Painting. This direction is particularly relevant for Cell Painting, where recent reviews highlight the potential of morphology-based phenotypic profiles, especially when combined with ML/DL models, for compound activity and hazard prediction [21]. Additionally, conducting a deeper toxicological analysis of the mapping results will help uncover historical toxicity trends and refine our understanding of compound-specific adverse outcomes. Finally, while this study focused on locally deployable open-weight models to ensure computational reproducibility, future work should compare this framework's performance against state-of-the-art private-source LLMs to benchmark the upper limits of automated biological reasoning.
