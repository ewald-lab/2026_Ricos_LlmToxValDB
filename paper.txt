Automated Ontology Annotation of Phenotypic Descriptions using Large Language Models (LLMs)

Abstract
1-Introduction
Biomedical studies worldwide generate immense volumes of phenotypic observations, creating unprecedented opportunities for cross-study integration and predictive modelling. However, across diverse biological domains, these observations often remain difficult to leverage within modern machine learning (ML) frameworks because they are predominantly recorded as unstructured free text in study reports [16]. To support supervised machine learning and quantitative comparisons across studies, these text-based descriptions must first be mapped to structured, machine-readable representations using a controlled vocabulary [23]. Traditionally, mapping unstructured text to standardised terms has relied on manual expert curation [29, 30]. This process is not scalable, limiting the size and diversity of datasets available for downstream model training and leaving the vast majority of phenotypic records unmapped.
Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) provide a promising solution to this scalability challenge. In biocuration, generative models and zero-shot learning frameworks have increasingly been applied to automate text normalisation to controlled vocabularies. For instance, GOFlowLLM uses flowchart-guided LLM reasoning to curate miRNA functional annotations against Gene Ontology terms [13], while SPIRES applies zero-shot structured prompting to extract and ground biological entities to ontologies directly from scientific text [25]. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [3] approaches extend these capabilities by combining semantic retrieval with language model reasoning to normalise biomedical text to standardised terminologies [5,6]. Non-generative hybrid methods have also advanced phenotype concept recognition beyond traditional dictionary-based methods; for example, PhenoTagger integrates dictionary lookup with deep learning to recognise Human Phenotype Ontology concepts in biomedical text [18]. More recently, integrated workflows combining semantic retrieval, LLM-based refinement, and expert curation have been applied to map Key Events in Adverse Outcome Pathways to gene annotations [26]. However, existing frameworks face limitations, as exhaustive LLM evaluation across large vocabularies is computationally prohibitive, the hierarchical structure of ontologies is not taken into account to ensure consistency among annotations, and ungrounded generative extraction risks introducing hallucinated terms.
Toxicology is one domain that generates rich phenotypic observations across cellular, tissue, and organismal levels. In a typical study, biological models are exposed to multiple compound concentrations to observe diverse endpoints. Traditionally, these endpoints are evaluated in animal models through histopathological and clinical findings, though the field is increasingly moving toward scalable New Approach Methodologies (NAMs) [28]. A major challenge in synthesising toxicological data is that studies report outcomes with varying levels of detail, ranging from broad terms like "liver toxicity" to specific cellular lesions. Without accounting for these differences in granularity, cross-study integration remains difficult, hindering efforts to assess data reproducibility or develop predictive machine learning models. Ontologies can help address this challenge because they define parent-child relationships between broad and specific terms, enabling comparisons across studies reported at different levels of resolution.
A prominent example of this challenge in toxicology is the Toxicity Values Database (ToxValDB) [1], a centralised resource developed by the U.S. EPA Center for Computational Toxicology and Exposure. ToxValDB integrates over 100,000 in vivo experimental results from diverse regulatory and research sources, harmonising experimental metadata across studies. However, the toxicological effect field describing the observed experimental outcome remains recorded as unstructured free text. Because terminology and reporting conventions vary across sources, this field creates a major bottleneck for predictive toxicology and NAM integration, such as linking dose responses to Cell Painting profiles [21]. Recent LLM-curated resources like UniTox have demonstrated the value of structuring regulatory text for downstream prediction tasks [17]. Yet, such resources typically assign broad drug- or organ-level ratings, whereas ToxValDB requires effect-level normalisation to biologically specific ontology terms. Developing a scalable, hierarchy-aware framework to map these free-text effects to controlled ontology terms is therefore essential for unlocking ToxValDB for predictive modelling.
To address this gap, we developed a hierarchy-aware framework powered by RAG and LLMs to convert free-text phenotypic descriptions into machine-readable ontology annotations (Figure 1). First, a RAG retrieval stage bypasses exhaustive term evaluation by targeting candidate terms directly at the relevant level of granularity, efficiently reducing the search space to a compact pool. An LLM then validates the retrieved candidates by considering their definitions and positions within the ontology hierarchy using a structured prompt design. Accepted annotations are automatically propagated to ancestor terms while queuing child terms to test for more specific matches, whereas rejected candidates trigger evaluation of parent terms, ensuring hierarchy-consistent mappings. This ontology-agnostic design allows the same workflow to be adapted to different controlled vocabularies with minimal modification. We demonstrate the framework on ToxValDB, mapping more than 16,000 unique toxicological effect descriptions to the Mammalian Phenotype (MP) ontology [2] (selected as the reference ontology) with an estimated >100-fold speedup relative to manual curation. Accuracy was assessed using a representative subset manually annotated by domain experts. LLM-generated mappings showed high similarity to the human expert consensus reference, with expert-LLM overlap comparable to, and often higher than, expert-expert overlap. Together with the high inter-model overlap observed across LLM backbones, these results support the framework as a scalable and reproducible approach for standardising complex biomedical free text.

2-Methods
2.1 Retrieval-Augmented Ontology Mapping
We implemented a two-stage retrieve-and-rerank pipeline followed by hierarchy-aware LLM validation to map toxicological effect descriptions to ontology terms (Figure 1B). Input descriptions and ontology term labels were embedded using a general-purpose embedding model [7], with ontology terms represented by their labels only to preserve comparability with short phrase-like input descriptions. Cosine similarity was used to retrieve the top K1 candidate ontology terms exceeding similarity threshold α. To capture fine-grained semantic distinctions missed by independent bi-encoder encoding, retrieved candidates were reranked using a cross-encoder that jointly encoded each description–candidate term pair [8]. The top K2 candidates with relevance scores above threshold ε were retained.
Retained candidates were evaluated by an LLM using ontology definitions and hierarchical context. For each candidate, the model generated a structured explanation supporting its decision. If a candidate was rejected, its parent terms were submitted for reranking and evaluation. If accepted, the annotation was propagated to its ancestor terms and its immediate child terms were evaluated in the next iteration. This bidirectional routing preserves broad biological validity while progressively refining mappings towards the most specific ontology level supported by the source text.
2.2 Reference Ontology
The Mammalian Phenotype Ontology (MP) [2] was selected as the reference ontology for mapping toxicological effect descriptions (Supplementary Table 1). When ontology terms lacked definitions, or existing definitions were incomplete, auxiliary definitions were generated using an LLM based on the term label and its hierarchical context (Supplementary Figure 1).
2.3 Case Study: ToxValDB Processing
The pipeline was applied to ToxValDB version 9.7.0 [1] using the toxicological effect field. Prior to ontology mapping, descriptions containing multiple effects were split into separate records. Rule-based filters removed procedural terminology, quantitative measurements, and species or sex modifiers retained elsewhere as structured metadata. A final LLM-based filtering step removed any remaining non-biological phrases before mapping.
2.4 Retrieval and Reranking Optimisation
Retrieval and reranking parameters were optimised independently of LLM validation using an internal test set of 50 manually annotated toxicological effect descriptions. To maximise semantic diversity, standardised effect embeddings were projected into two dimensions using principal component analysis (PCA), clustered with K-means, and the description closest to each cluster centroid was selected. Although this 2D projection captures only a fraction of high-dimensional embedding variance, it served effectively as a diversity-oriented sampling strategy [10]. This optimisation set did not overlap with the final external test set.
Four embedding models from the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB) leaderboard [11] were evaluated (Supplementary Table 2). Candidate reranking was performed using models from the Qwen3-Reranker family (Supplementary Table 2) [8]. Hyperparameters were optimised using a grid search over retrieval and reranking settings (Supplementary Table 3, Supplementary Figures 2–3). The final configuration used Qwen3-Embedding-8B together with Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B, achieving a recall of 0.95 with a context burden of 20 candidate ontology terms.
2.5 Test Set Design
Pipeline performance was evaluated using an independent test set of 50 semantically diverse toxicological effect descriptions generated using the same PCA and K-means sampling strategy. Candidate ontology terms were generated using the optimised retrieval pipeline. For manual evaluation, the candidate set was restricted to the top 10 ontology terms per description.
A panel of six independent toxicology experts across agrochemical, pharmaceutical, non-profit, and consulting institutions reviewed each candidate set using a structured annotation form (Supplementary Figures 4–6). Experts were instructed to select all ontology terms representing accurate biological matches using instructions equivalent to those provided to the LLMs (Supplementary Material S1). Consensus annotations were defined as ontology terms selected by at least two experts, excluding reviewers who considered a description uninterpretable or outside their expertise. Unless otherwise stated, both expert and LLM annotations were propagated to ontology ancestor terms before performance metrics were calculated (Supplementary Material S2).
2.6 Hardware Requirements
Nine open-weight LLMs representing a range of architectures, model generations, and parameter sizes were evaluated (Supplementary Table 4). Inference parameters, including temperature, context length, and reasoning settings, are provided in Supplementary Material S3.
All models were deployed on the EMBL-EBI High-Performance Computing (HPC) cluster using vLLM [12]. The pipeline also supports external APIs for closed-source models. In addition to the LLM server, a second GPU was used for embedding generation and candidate reranking.
2.7 Prompt Design 
Task-specific prompts were used throughout the pipeline. Sequential preprocessing prompts standardised toxicological descriptions by separating multiple effects, removing non-biological content and metadata, and harmonising terminology. Additional prompts generated auxiliary ontology definitions, supported candidate reranking, and performed ontology term validation in both batch and single-term modes (Supplementary Material S4).


3-Results
3.1 LLM annotations align with experts and form stable annotator profiles

We focused our human benchmark on the LLM validation step, the most challenging component of the pipeline, as evaluating term accuracy and selecting appropriate ontology granularity require subtle biological reasoning. To determine how similar LLM-generated mappings were to expert annotations on these decisions, we quantitatively compared model outputs against an external panel of experts using pairwise Jaccard similarity [24] and hierarchical clustering.

To establish baseline agreement among human experts, we first analyzed phrase-level expert curation (Supplementary Figure 7). Human annotations showed substantial phrase-dependent differences, confirming that mapping toxicological free text to MP terms is not always a uniquely defined task. In many cases, different experts selected related but non-identical ontology terms, reflecting differences in biological interpretation, preferred ontology granularity, or the level of specificity considered supported by the source text.

When comparing per-phrase human-human and LLM-LLM Jaccard similarity, we observed a significant positive correlation, indicating that toxicological effects with higher concordance among experts also tended to show higher agreement among LLMs (Figure 2A). This suggests that both humans and LLMs are sensitive to a shared underlying axis of annotation ambiguity, rather than producing unrelated disagreement patterns.

Across comparison groups, LLM-LLM Jaccard similarity (0.83 ± 0.15) was 1.5 times higher than human-human similarity (0.54 ± 0.23; p = 1.0 × 10⁻¹¹, paired t-test), indicating that different model backbones converged on consistent ontology mappings under the same validation framework. Human-LLM Jaccard similarity (0.63 ± 0.21) was also significantly higher than human-human similarity (p = 4.9 × 10⁻⁶, paired t-test), demonstrating that LLM annotations conformed at least as closely to expert judgment as individual experts aligned with one another (Figure 2B).

We then asked whether these agreement patterns translated into stable annotator-level profiles. Hierarchical clustering based on pairwise annotation overlap revealed that LLMs generally formed a compact group, indicating that different model backbones produced similar ontology annotation profiles under the same mapping framework (Figure 2C). Models from the same provider also tended to cluster together, suggesting that shared model architecture or training lineage may contribute to subtle but consistent annotation preferences. By contrast, human experts showed greater overall dispersion across the dendrogram. Notably, experts with shared institutional backgrounds clustered tightly together (Experts 2, 3, and 6), reflecting shared curation practices, whereas Expert 1 grouped closely with the LLM group and others (Experts 4 and 5) exhibited distinct individual annotation profiles.

These annotator-level profiles were also reflected in ontology-term usage (Figure 2D). Human experts varied substantially, with Expert 4 choosing fewer, broader terms at shallow ontology levels (mean 1.8 terms per effect; mean depth 1.9), whereas Expert 5 selected more expansive annotations reaching deeper ontology levels (mean 11.3 terms per effect; mean depth 5.1). The remaining experts (Experts 1, 2, 3, and 6) chose intermediate term sets (mean 5.7–6.9 terms; mean depth 3.9–4.4). In comparison, LLMs exhibited a highly consistent term-usage profile across model families, selecting a similar number of terms (mean 6.5–9.0 terms per effect) and reaching comparable ontology depths (mean depth 3.8–5.1). These metrics confirm that human variation stems from individual preferences in ontology depth, whereas LLMs maintain consistent annotation granularity.

Overall, these results suggest that disagreement in this annotation task is largely driven by the biological and semantic ambiguity of the toxicological descriptions themselves, together with systematic annotator-specific preferences in ontology granularity and coverage, rather than by inconsistent LLM behaviour. The correspondence between human and LLM agreement across phrases, the high inter-model overlap, and the coherent LLM annotation profile support the framework as a reproducible approach for ontology-based standardisation of complex biomedical free text.

3.2 Impact of LLM configuration and model backbone on ontology-mapping performance

To investigate how model configuration influenced ontology-mapping performance, we evaluated three experimental parameters: candidate-evaluation mode, inclusion of ontology definitions, and the number of independent LLM consensus runs (1, 3, 5, or 10 generations, where ontology terms selected in at least 50% of the runs were retained). We observed that batched evaluation, in which the LLM assessed the full candidate pool jointly, improved agreement with the human expert consensus reference compared with single-term evaluation, where each candidate was assessed independently (p = 3.3 × 10⁻¹⁴, paired t-test; Figure 3). Including ontology definitions produced a smaller benefit (p = 9.6 × 10⁻⁴, paired t-test), and increasing the number of consensus runs did not yield substantial improvements (p = 0.31, Friedman test). Together, these results suggest that candidate-evaluation mode has the clearest effect on performance, as access to the full candidate pool helps the LLM compare closely related ontology terms and select an appropriate level of specificity. Furthermore, they indicate that a single structured validation run is sufficient under the selected prompt design. The most efficient configuration therefore combined batched evaluation, ontology definitions, and a single LLM consensus run.


Figure 3. Impact of LLM configuration parameters on ontology-mapping performance. F1 scores against the human expert consensus reference were compared across ontology-definition settings (left), candidate-evaluation modes (middle), and consensus-run settings (right) using the external human-annotated test set. Each point represents the F1 score of a single LLM under a specific parameter combination, meaning each plotted category aggregates model performance across all variations of the remaining unplotted parameters. Statistical significance was assessed using a paired t-test for the definition and candidate-evaluation mode comparisons, and a Friedman test for the number of consensus runs.

We next evaluated the influence of the underlying model by comparing performance across LLM backbones using this selected configuration. We observed that most models maintained a broadly similar overall F1 range (Figure 4), with several smaller variants achieving competitive results relative to their larger counterparts, despite modest family-specific differences in the balance between precision, recall, and ontology overlap. This overall similarity in performance partly reflects the high baseline variability among human annotators in the reference standard, which inherently constrained our ability to sharply discriminate between subtle performance differences across backbones. Similarly, the average number of generated tokens did not vary substantially across backbones. Based on this, we conclude that the pipeline is not dependent on a single model family. For the final large-scale annotation pipeline, we selected Qwen3.6-35B-A3B because it provided the highest F1 score and Jaccard Overlap among models that fit on a single 80 GB GPU, and there was not a large enough improvement for the multi-GPU alternatives to justify the additional computational cost. Furthermore, we opted for open-weight models rather than proprietary closed-source LLMs via API, as open-source models achieved sufficiently high performance while providing key advantages in reproducibility, data privacy, and full control over computational throughput.

3.3 The pipeline scales to full ToxValDB annotation with high coverage

To evaluate real-world scalability, we deployed the selected framework across the full ToxValDB in vivo toxicological effect corpus using the Mammalian Phenotype (MP) ontology. The system was hosted on a two-GPU node, with one 80 GB GPU serving the FP8-quantised validation LLM (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B) and a second GPU handling the RAG system. We used the FP8-quantised model after confirming that quantisation had a negligible impact on validation accuracy compared with the unquantised model (see Supplementary Figure 8). By parallelising requests, the pipeline processed approximately 17,000 unique effect descriptions in under three hours. Given that manual curation by domain experts proceeds at approximately 50 records per hour (requiring an estimated 340 hours of expert labor for the full corpus), this automated execution represents a >100-fold speedup, supporting the framework's suitability for large-scale database standardisation.

The pipeline mapped 97.8% (16,356) of unique toxicological effect descriptions to at least one MP term, leaving only 2.2% (367) unmapped (excluding the redundant root term, mammalian phenotype). This high coverage indicates that most historical ToxValDB effects can be represented using existing MP phenotype terms. Most accepted term mappings (86.3%, 157,255) originated directly from the RAG candidate pool, while iterative hierarchical routing identified the remaining 13.7% (24,989). This shows that evaluating parent and child terms during validation recovered biologically valid annotations that were not captured among the first-pass RAG candidates.

Qualitative inspection of the 2.2% unmapped descriptions revealed that these unassigned entries primarily represent non-effect descriptions or non-biological records that bypassed upstream preprocessing filtering. They fall into four main categories: (i) negative or normal clinical findings explicitly recording an absence of effect (e.g. "no adverse ophthalmic effects observed", "respiratory rate: no adverse effect"); (ii) pharmacokinetic and exposure parameters rather than phenotypic endpoints (e.g. "increased AUC", "drug plasma levels increased"); (iii) highly generic phrases lacking organ context or lesion specificity (e.g. "effect on organ weights", "histopathology: nonneoplastic"); and (iv) administrative artifacts or report noise (e.g. study metadata blocks or generation labels). These findings indicate that the overall mapping coverage is primarily limited by upstream data quality and preprocessing constraints, rather than LLM capabilities or limitations of the target ontology.

The distribution of the maximum ontology depth assigned per toxicological effect (Figure 5A) shows that the mappings reached deep levels of the ontology hierarchy, indicating that the framework successfully resolved descriptions to specific phenotypic terms rather than broad, top-level concepts. In particular, only a minor fraction of the effects were mapped to shallow terms (depth 1 or 2), whereas the vast majority of annotations reached deeper levels (depths 4 to 6), peaking at depth 5. This demonstrates that the LLM-driven validation step retains biologically precise annotations when supported by the source text.

We also analyzed how mapped effects were distributed across level-2 branches of the MP ontology to understand the main biological domains represented in ToxValDB (Figure 5B). First, most reported effects mapped to systemic or metabolic disruptions (such as body weight changes or altered clinical chemistry) rather than isolated anatomical lesions. Second, organ-specific toxicity was heavily concentrated in major regulatory and metabolic systems, particularly the liver, kidney, endocrine, and immune systems. Third, sensory and developmental phenotypes were the least frequently mapped categories. This pattern directly reflects standard regulatory toxicology testing, which routinely monitors systemic pathology and major organ weights in repeated-dose animal studies, but rarely evaluates specialized sensory or developmental endpoints.

Finally, we assessed whether LLM-assigned ontology depth reflected historical differences in toxicological reporting practices across study publication years (Figure 5C). We observe that studies before ~1960 show a lower mean maximum depth, indicating that earlier records tend to support less specific phenotype annotations. This temporal pattern suggests that the pipeline successfully adjusted annotation granularity according to the detail of the source text, reflecting the fact that older toxicity studies were often reported under less standardised and less stringent documentation practices. This underscores why mapping terms to a hierarchical ontology is important, because it allows us to compare newer, more precisely described toxicological effects to historical data.

4-Discussion
4.1 Framework Performance And Annotator Consensus
The integration of legacy toxicological data into modern machine learning workflows remains severely bottlenecked by the unstructured free text of historical study reports. Recent LLM-supported SR-Domain workflows have addressed the complementary upstream task of extracting expert toxicological interpretations from full preclinical study reports into SEND-aligned structured records [16]. Other initiatives have successfully combined semantic similarity matching, LLM-based filtering, and expert curation to map toxicological Key Events in Adverse Outcome Pathways (AOPs) to ontology and pathway terms [26]. By contrast, the unique strength of the LLM-driven pipeline presented in this study lies in its active exploitation of the ontology's hierarchical structure during retrieval and validation. This hierarchy-aware validation approach enables automated downstream normalisation that maps already curated toxicological effect descriptions to biologically expressive Mammalian Phenotype annotations with an estimated 100-fold speedup compared with manual curation.
A central finding of this study is the strong alignment observed across annotator groups, with human-human overlap consistently lower than both LLM-LLM and human-LLM overlap. The higher Jaccard similarity observed among different LLM backbones suggests that language models converge on a stable and reproducible annotation profile when operating under a unified reasoning framework. Notably, the finding that human-LLM agreement surpassed human-human agreement indicates that the automated framework can approximate the operational expert consensus defined in this study, aligning with individual specialist judgements at least as well as, and in some cases better than, specialists align with each other.
This interpretation should be considered in the context of the limited test-set size and the construction of the human reference. Expert-derived reference standards are often pragmatic aggregations of annotator judgements rather than perfect gold standards, and should be interpreted alongside inter-annotator agreement [24]. Accordingly, the human expert consensus reference used here reflects expert agreement within a shared candidate pool rather than an absolute ground truth. Nevertheless, the observed agreement patterns support the view that much of the annotation divergence arises from the semantic ambiguity of the biological source text, rather than from erratic model behaviour. Indeed, this substantial level of annotator variance is consistent with prior evidence in manual biocuration, where inter-curator disagreement remains a well-documented challenge [29, 30]. Together, these findings position the LLM framework as a reproducible approach for reducing individual annotator-specific variation in ontology-based standardisation and for generating structured phenotype labels suitable for downstream computational toxicology.




4.2 Application To Other Domains
Although this framework was developed to standardise preclinical toxicological effects across biological ontologies, the underlying architecture is inherently domain-agnostic. The only steps strictly tailored to toxicology in our pipeline were the initial data pre-processing (which filtered non-effect entries and administrative metadata specific to ToxValDB) and the specific criteria encoded in the LLM system prompt detailing what constitutes a valid biological match. Consequently, the pipeline can be easily adapted to other scientific domains by simply adjusting the input data, target ontology, and prompt design. This general approach should be broadly applicable for standardising unstructured text to any hierarchical ontology with minimal adaptation.
4.2 Limitations And Future Directions
A primary limitation of the current framework is its focus on short, isolated phenotypic descriptions. This design reflects the nature of the ToxValDB dataset, where the effect descriptions had already been manually extracted from primary sources by curators. Consequently, the pipeline is not designed to extract and map observations directly from full-length scientific articles or toxicology study reports. Domain-specific systems such as PretoxTM show that extracting treatment-related findings from preclinical toxicology reports is feasible, but also that report-level extraction requires dedicated document parsing, section detection, entity normalisation, relation extraction, and expert validation [19]. Extending our framework to complete manuscripts will therefore require more advanced information extraction strategies. For example, flowchart-guided approaches such as GOFlowLLM [13] could improve extraction of specific biological outcomes from complex text, while recent visual retrieval-augmented generation models such as PixelRAG [14] may facilitate processing of full scientific documents by operating directly on page images, preserving layout and tabular information. Additionally, advanced document parsing systems like Mistral OCR 4 [15] can complement these efforts by translating complex layouts, multi-column text, and mathematical notations from raw documents into structured, citation-ready block primitives.
A separate limitation arises from the candidate-based annotation design. Because both human experts and LLMs evaluated the same retrieved candidates, the comparison provides a controlled assessment of ontology-term validation, but does not fully measure cases in which the most appropriate ontology term was absent from the candidate pool. Similarly, ancestor expansion provides a biologically meaningful way to capture semantic overlap between related ontology terms, but may partially obscure differences in annotation specificity by increasing agreement between broad and narrow mappings. This trade-off is consistent with broader work on semantic similarity in biomedical ontologies, where graph-based comparison enables biologically meaningful relaxed matching but remains sensitive to ontology depth, density, and annotation specificity [20]. Finally, the use of LLM-generated auxiliary ontology definitions improved coverage for sparsely defined terms, but these generated definitions may introduce model-specific bias or unsupported assumptions. Future implementations should therefore version, audit, and, where possible, manually review auxiliary definitions, especially for high-impact or frequently selected ontology terms.
Looking forward, several avenues exist to expand the utility and biological impact of this framework. A critical next step is to utilise these newly standardised toxicological labels as structured target variables to train predictive machine learning models in computational toxicology. High-quality, ontology-grounded annotations are essential for developing robust predictive models capable of inferring compound hazard and activity profiles. Additionally, conducting a deeper toxicological analysis of the mapping results will help uncover historical toxicity trends and refine our understanding of compound-specific adverse outcomes. Beyond standardising unstructured text, our findings highlight broader applications for LLMs in ontology management. For instance, similar retrieval and validation approaches could be employed to map terms across different ontologies, effectively "translating" between diverse biological vocabularies, or serve as an automated diagnostic tool to identify areas where an ontology requires structural refinement or expanded coverage. Finally, given the rapid pace of language model development, where reasoning and cognitive capabilities increase substantially over short timeframes, future work should continuously track these advancements across both open-source and proprietary architectures to benchmark the evolving limits of automated biological reasoning.
